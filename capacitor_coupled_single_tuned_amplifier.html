1. Introduction and learning objectives

A single-tuned amplifier is a tuned RF amplifier that uses one parallel LC resonant circuit as its frequency-selective load. In a common-emitter transistor version, capacitors couple the AC input/output (and may couple successive stages) while blocking DC between sections. The amplifier gives its largest voltage gain near the tuned resonant frequency.

Core idea: the transistor provides amplification; the parallel L–C tank selects a narrow band of frequencies around resonance.

Learning objectives

  • Identify the function of coupling, biasing and bypass components.
  • Explain DC and AC operation separately.
  • Calculate resonant frequency, bandwidth and Q-factor.
  • Interpret gain-versus-frequency response.
  • Apply the circuit to RF receiver and communication examples.

Prerequisites

  • BJT common-emitter amplifier basics
  • Capacitor blocks steady DC and passes changing signals
  • Inductive and capacitive reactance
  • Resonance, impedance, gain and decibels

3. Working principle: step by step

  1. DC bias: Cᵢₙ and Cₒᵤₜ behave approximately as open circuits after steady state. The bias network establishes the transistor's quiescent operating point.
  2. Input coupling: an AC signal passes through Cᵢₙ to the base while the source's DC is isolated.
  3. Transistor action: small base-current/voltage variations cause collector-current variations.
  4. Tuned load: the collector current excites the parallel LC network. Around resonance, its impedance magnitude is high, so the collector voltage variation and gain are large.
  5. Frequency selection: well below or above resonance, the tank impedance decreases, so gain falls.
  6. Output coupling: Cₒᵤₜ transfers the AC output to the load while blocking the collector's DC voltage.

At resonance

For an ideal tank, XL = XC. Inductive and capacitive susceptances cancel. The parallel tank presents maximum impedance (ideally infinite); in a real circuit, losses and loading make it finite.

Off resonance

The inductive and capacitive effects no longer cancel. Tank impedance falls, reducing output voltage and gain. This creates frequency selectivity.

4. Mathematical analysis and derivations

4.1 Resonant frequency

At resonance, the magnitude of inductive reactance equals capacitive reactance:

XL = XC   ⇒   2πf₀L = 1/(2πf₀C)

Multiplying both sides by 2πf₀C and rearranging gives:

f₀ = 1 / (2π√LC)

Here f₀ is in hertz (Hz), L is inductance in henries (H), and C is capacitance in farads (F). Use the effective tank capacitance, including relevant parasitic capacitance where known.

4.2 Parallel tank impedance

For an ideal parallel LC tank, admittance is:

Y(jω) = jωC + 1/(jωL) = j(ωC − 1/(ωL))

At ω₀ = 1/√LC, the imaginary terms cancel. The ideal impedance tends to infinity. With an effective parallel loss resistance Rₚ, the impedance at resonance is approximately Rₚ, and loading reduces it further.

4.3 Voltage gain

For a common-emitter stage, a useful small-signal approximation is:

Aᵥ(f) ≈ −gₘ · Zload(f)

where gₘ is transistor transconductance in siemens (S), and Zload(f) is the frequency-dependent effective collector load including the tank, transistor output resistance and external loading. The minus sign indicates the approximate 180° phase inversion of a common-emitter stage. This is a simplified model; the exact gain depends on the circuit topology and parasitics.

4.4 Bandwidth and quality factor

BW = fH − fL    and    Q ≈ f₀/BW

fL and fH are the lower and upper half-power frequencies. The Q relation applies to a resonant response when the bandwidth is defined consistently around the resonance; for a narrow-band resonator, f₀ is often approximated by the midpoint of fL and fH.

4.5 Decibels

Gain (dB) = 20 log₁₀ |Aᵥ|

For a voltage gain magnitude of 10, the gain is 20 dB. A −3 dB point corresponds to a voltage magnitude of about 0.707 of the peak if the measurement impedance is unchanged.

What controls selectivity?

  • Higher Q generally means a narrower bandwidth and sharper tuning.
  • Higher load or lower loss can increase tank impedance and gain, but the actual circuit's Q is affected by loading.
  • Changing L or C changes f₀; increasing either one lowers the resonant frequency if the other is held constant.

6. Solved numerical problems

Example 1 — Calculate resonant frequency (easy)

Given: L = 100 μH and C = 100 pF. Find f₀.

Formula: f₀ = 1/(2π√LC)

Substitute: LC = (100 × 10⁻⁶)(100 × 10⁻¹²) = 10⁻¹⁴; √LC = 10⁻⁷.

f₀ = 1 / (2π × 10⁻⁷) ≈ 1.5915 × 10⁶ Hz

Answer: f₀ ≈ 1.59 MHz. The amplifier is tuned to a radio-frequency signal near this frequency, subject to real-world parasitics.

Example 2 — Bandwidth and Q (moderate)

Given: f₀ = 2 MHz, fL = 1.95 MHz and fH = 2.05 MHz. Find bandwidth and Q.

BW = fH − fL = 2.05 − 1.95 = 0.10 MHz = 100 kHz
Q ≈ f₀/BW = 2 MHz / 0.10 MHz = 20

Answer: bandwidth = 100 kHz, Q ≈ 20. The response is relatively selective around 2 MHz.

Example 3 — Find C for a target frequency (GATE-style)

Given: L = 25 μH and desired f₀ = 4 MHz. Find C, neglecting parasitic capacitance.

Rearrange f₀ = 1/(2π√LC):

C = 1 / [(2πf₀)²L]

Substitute:

C = 1 / [(2π × 4 × 10⁶)² × 25 × 10⁻⁶] ≈ 63.3 × 10⁻¹² F

Answer: C ≈ 63.3 pF. A practical design may require a slightly different component value after including parasitic capacitance and adjusting the tank.

Numerical shortcut: use consistent SI units first. A convenient approximate relation is f₀(Hz) = 1/[2π√(L(H)C(F))]. Do not enter μH or pF as if they were H or F.

7. Applications, advantages and limitations

Applications

  • RF stages in radio receivers and transmitters
  • Frequency-selective amplification in communication equipment
  • Intermediate-frequency or tuned front-end stages where a narrow band is required
  • Laboratory demonstrations of resonance and selectivity
  • Signal-conditioning stages where a known carrier frequency is amplified

Advantages

  • Provides high gain near a chosen frequency.
  • Rejects signals sufficiently far from resonance.
  • Passive LC tank is conceptually simple.
  • Resonant frequency can be adjusted by varying L or C.

Limitations and practical challenges

  • It is narrow-band; it is not suitable when a wide, flat frequency response is required.
  • Inductor size, tolerance, series resistance and parasitic capacitance affect tuning.
  • Loading by the next stage, probe or antenna changes gain and Q.
  • Temperature drift and component tolerance can shift f₀.
  • High gain at resonance may cause unwanted oscillation if feedback and layout are not controlled.

Practical testing checklist

  1. Verify the DC bias point before applying a large AC input.
  2. Use a small input signal to avoid clipping or transistor nonlinearity.
  3. Sweep frequency across the expected resonance and record output amplitude.
  4. Find f₀ at peak gain, then fL and fH at the −3 dB points.
  5. Calculate BW and Q; compare measured values with theoretical values.
  6. Check probe loading, grounding, component values and instrument settings if results differ.

9. Core electronics interview questions

Open each question to review a concise interview-ready answer.

1. What is a single-tuned amplifier?

An amplifier using one tuned LC circuit as a load to provide high gain around one resonant frequency and lower gain away from it.

2. Why is it called “single tuned”?

Because one resonant LC tank determines the principal tuned response of the stage. Double-tuned amplifiers use two coupled tuned circuits.

3. What is the role of the coupling capacitor?

It passes the changing AC signal and blocks DC, preventing one stage's bias voltage from directly disturbing another stage.

4. Derive the resonant-frequency formula.

At resonance, 2πf₀L = 1/(2πf₀C). Rearranging gives f₀ = 1/(2π√LC).

5. Why is the gain maximum at resonance?

The parallel tank's impedance magnitude is maximum near resonance, so collector-current variations produce a larger collector-voltage variation.

6. What is Q-factor?

Q measures resonator selectivity. For a narrow resonant response, Q ≈ f₀/BW; higher Q generally means narrower bandwidth.

7. How would you measure bandwidth in a lab?

Sweep the input frequency, record output amplitude, identify peak gain, then find the two frequencies where voltage gain falls to about 0.707 of peak (−3 dB). Their difference is BW.

8. Why might measured f₀ differ from the calculated value?

Component tolerances, coil self-capacitance, transistor junction capacitances, wiring, probe loading and the next-stage load can shift resonance.

9. What happens if the load is connected directly and heavily loads the tank?

Effective tank impedance and Q may decrease, reducing peak gain and broadening the response. Buffering can reduce loading.

10. What practical issue can cause a distorted output?

Excessive input amplitude can drive the transistor out of its small-signal region or into cutoff/saturation. Check bias, input amplitude, supply and instrument settings.

11. How do L and C tune the stage?

f₀ is inversely proportional to √(LC). Increasing L or C alone lowers the resonant frequency; decreasing either raises it.

12. What is one advantage and one limitation compared with a wideband amplifier?

Advantage: strong frequency selectivity near the chosen carrier. Limitation: it amplifies only a limited band and can be sensitive to tuning drift.

Industry perspective

RF hardware engineers use tuned stages to select or amplify signals in receiver front ends and other narrowband circuits. In practice they combine calculations with SPICE/Multisim simulation, impedance measurements, frequency sweeps, component-tolerance analysis and careful PCB layout.

Five points to remember

  1. Transistor = amplification; LC tank = frequency selection.
  2. At parallel resonance, tank impedance is ideally maximum.
  3. Coupling capacitors pass AC and isolate DC bias.
  4. BW = fH − fL and Q ≈ f₀/BW.
  5. Real losses, loading and parasitics change the ideal result.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the square root in f₀ = 1/(2π√LC).
  • Using microhenries/picofarads without converting to H/F.
  • Confusing parallel resonance (high impedance) with series resonance (low impedance).
  • Calling f₀/BW the exact Q for every heavily loaded or nonstandard response without checking assumptions.
  • Assuming a real circuit's tank impedance is infinite at resonance.
